# #ThinkRoger Editorial Framework

## Core Promise

> Make complex digital change understandable without making it simplistic.

#ThinkRoger treats every post as an argument, not a content summary. The reader should understand what happened, how it works, why it matters, and how to think or decide differently.

## Topic Architecture

| Pillar | Editorial Questions |
|---|---|
| AI and human agency | Who decides, who benefits, what becomes automated, and where must human judgement remain? |
| Algorithms and power | How do ranking, recommendation, scoring, and optimisation shape behaviour, opportunity, and visibility? |
| Digital business models | Where is value created, captured, or shifted, and which incentives explain the visible product? |
| Leadership and governance | What must leaders understand, measure, govern, or refuse to delegate? |
| Education and AI literacy | Which capabilities help people question, verify, use, and govern digital systems? |
| Work and organisational design | Which tasks, roles, workflows, and decision rights change when AI enters the system? |
| Technology and society | Which second-order effects, dependencies, and trade-offs are being ignored? |
| Digital sovereignty | Which choices preserve autonomy, contestability, privacy, resilience, and meaningful control? |
| Hype versus evidence | What is demonstrated, inferred, forecast, or merely marketed? |
| Global perspectives | How do geography, regulation, language, infrastructure, and culture change the conclusion? |

## Thinking Models

Use at least two models for each carousel. Record the selected models in the planning notes.

| Model | Use |
|---|---|
| Claim - Evidence - Implication | Separate the assertion, its support, and its consequence. |
| Incentives over intentions | Analyse what the system rewards, not only what actors say. |
| Mechanism, not metaphor | Explain how an effect is produced. |
| First-order and second-order effects | Distinguish immediate gains from later consequences. |
| Who benefits, who pays, who decides | Map power, distribution, and decision rights. |
| Capability versus adoption | Separate technical possibility from organisational implementation. |
| Efficiency versus agency | Test whether optimisation reduces meaningful human control. |
| Risk versus uncertainty | Separate measurable exposure from unknown or contested outcomes. |
| Local fact versus global relevance | Avoid treating one market, study, or company as universal evidence. |
| Reversible versus irreversible decisions | Distinguish safe experiments from structural lock-in. |
| Signal versus anecdote | Prevent a vivid example from becoming a false trend. |
| Counterfactual thinking | Ask what would happen without the intervention or technology. |

## Evidence Standard

Facts must come from supplied material or explicit research. Never invent a number, quote, date, causal claim, market estimate, legal statement, or study finding.

Use this source hierarchy when research is necessary:

1. Primary sources: laws, regulators, official statistics, original research, technical documentation, company filings, and direct statements.
2. Strong secondary sources: established research institutes, respected journalism, and recognised industry analysis.
3. Contextual sources: expert commentary and specialist publications.
4. Social posts and unsourced summaries: discovery only, never the sole support for a consequential claim.

For each consequential claim, record the source URL, publication date, and access date. Cross-check high-impact or disputed claims with a second independent source where possible.

## Epistemic Labels

| Status | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Verified fact | Directly supported by a credible source. |
| Reported claim | Attributed to a person or organisation, but not independently established. |
| Interpretation | A reasoned reading of the evidence. |
| Forecast | A future-oriented judgement with uncertainty. |
| Open question | Evidence is incomplete, contested, or unavailable. |

Use these labels in research notes. Do not overcrowd slides with them. Attribute reported claims directly. Never imply that correlation proves causation.

## International Relevance Test

Before drafting, ask whether the source represents a global pattern, a regional development, a single organisation, or an isolated case. Name the geography, population, sector, currency, and period when they materially affect the conclusion. Explain unfamiliar institutions instead of assuming knowledge.

## Editorial Failure Conditions

Reject or revise the carousel when the central claim cannot be traced to evidence, interpretation is presented as fact, a credible counterpoint is omitted, the framework is generic, urgency is manufactured, or an international audience would need unstated regional context to understand the point.
